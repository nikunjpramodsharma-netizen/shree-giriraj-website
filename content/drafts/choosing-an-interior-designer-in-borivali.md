## The problem with how interiors are usually quoted

Most quotes arrive as a single number for the whole flat. That number is impossible to compare against another single number, because you cannot see what either one contains.

A quote you can actually judge has three things in it: **quantities, specifications and exclusions.** Twelve running feet of wardrobe in a named board, not "bedroom wardrobes". Once you have that, two quotes become comparable and the cheaper one stops automatically looking better.

---

## The questions worth asking

### 1. What is the scope, in quantities?

Ask for running feet, square feet and unit counts. If a designer will not put quantities in writing before an advance, that is the answer to the question.

### 2. What is excluded?

This is where the budget actually goes wrong. Things that are commonly outside an interiors quote and land later as separate bills:

- Civil work, if walls are being moved
- Electrical load upgrades
- Plumbing changes beyond the existing points
- Loose furniture, curtains and appliances
- Society charges, debris removal and lift usage

### 3. Who is on site, and how often?

Design and execution are different jobs and are often different people. Ask who supervises day to day, how often they visit, and who you call when a carpenter has a question at 11am on a Tuesday. **A designer who visits weekly and a supervisor who is there daily is a different arrangement from a designer who visits weekly and nobody else.**

### 4. What happens if it runs late?

Interiors run late. The useful question is not whether it will, but what happens when it does. A written completion date with an agreed consequence is worth more than an optimistic verbal one.

### 5. What has this person built near here?

Ask to see a finished flat in a nearby building, not a rendering. Renderings are made by a different person than the one holding the drill.
